# An Imitation Learning Approach for Cache Replacement

## What this paper does
Parrot casts single-level CPU cache replacement as imitation learning of Belady's optimal policy. Belady's evicts the line whose next access is furthest in the future; it needs future accesses, so it is only an offline oracle. Because the access trace is fixed and the oracle can be queried at any cache state during training, Parrot trains a causal policy (LSTM over past accesses, attention from each resident line to the history) to reproduce the oracle's eviction ranking, using DAgger roll-outs, a ranking (NDCG) loss and an auxiliary reuse-distance head. It beats hand-designed heuristics and the prior learned-from-Belady classifiers (Hawkeye, Glider).

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is the core mechanism, and why does it work for cache replacement?
The oracle decision (evict argmax reuse distance) is computable exactly offline because future accesses are fixed, and querying it is cheap at any state, unlike typical IL experts [full text, Sections 2, 3, 4.2]. The learned policy only conditions on the current access, the resident lines and the past H accesses, and outputs a distribution over which resident line to evict [full text, Section 3, Section 4.1]. Decisions are made only at misses, among the currently resident lines plus the incoming one, so the policy ranks candidates rather than predicting a whole future set [full text, Section 3]. Prior heuristics (LRU, MRU, re-reference prediction) each fit one access pattern and fail on others [full text, Section 1]. Figure 2 shows matching 80% of Belady needs exact reuse distances 2600 accesses ahead, i.e. the gain is not reachable by short look-ahead, yet the learned causal policy closes a large part of it: Parrot exceeds the state of the art by over 20% hit rate on memory-intensive SPEC benchmarks and by 61% over LRU on web search [full text, Sections 1, 3, 5.2].
### Goal: Which training ingredients matter and what does each fix?
On-policy data collection with DAgger: training on oracle roll-outs causes compounding errors because the learned policy's own evictions create cache states never seen under the oracle; following the learned policy and relabeling with the oracle gives +9.8% normalized hit rate on average, though it is program-dependent [full text, Sections 4.2, 5.3]. A ranking loss over all resident lines (relevance = reuse distance) penalises evicting soon-reused lines more than near-optimal mistakes, +3.5% over log-likelihood [full text, Sections 4.3, 5.3]. An auxiliary head that regresses log reuse distance improves representations (+16.8%), and works better as an auxiliary loss than as the eviction rule itself, because ranking is easier than exact distance prediction [full text, Sections 4.4, 5.3]. History beyond ~80 past accesses stops helping [full text, Section 5.4].
### Goal: What assumption does the mechanism rely on, and what does it report as its limitation?
It assumes the oracle's future-dependent choice is partly predictable from past accesses and current residents, and that the oracle can be evaluated on states produced by the learned policy. The stated limits are deployment cost: model size and latency may overshadow the hit-rate gain; the authors propose distillation, pruning and quantization of the policy and note that software caches tolerate more latency [full text, Sections 4.5, 7]. No optimal oracle exists for multi-level caches [full text, Section 7].
