# Random Attention: Rethinking KV Cache Eviction for Efficient Reasoning

## What this paper does
Tests whether the selection score of KV eviction matters for reasoning models. Random Attention pins the entire prompt and keeps a uniformly random top-K of the generated trace independently per KV head, with no scoring pass. Across Qwen3-4B/14B/32B and Phi-4-reasoning on six math/science/code tasks it matches the strongest baselines (SnapKV, R-KV, VaSE, TriAttention) and serves 32-43% more tokens/s in vLLM. Controlled studies attribute most baseline differences to prompt protection and show the reasoning trace is redundant in text and across heads; the residual case for a selection signal is a once-stated fact needed much later.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: What signal does it use to decide which KV entries to keep, evict, or recall, and is the decision reversible?
No signal: s_i = +inf for prompt positions and s_i ~ Uniform(0,1) otherwise, drawn independently per KV head at every periodic eviction, then top-K [full text, Section 3]. Eviction is permanent.

### Goal: What does it report as its failure mode or limitation for tokens that are needed later (long chain-of-thought or long context)?
Once every method protects the prompt, SnapKV, R-KV and VaSE land within 2.2 points of one another and do not beat random; on Qwen3-4B they trail random by 4-6 points [Section 5.1, Table 2]. A planted-fact probe shows cross-head pooling is strongly superadditive: the best single head retrieves the fact in 3% of trials, two heads together 60%, three 83%, all eight 99%; the shape of surviving copies does not matter [Section 5.2, Fig 4]. The residual failure: a passcode stated once 57 compression rounds before the question is retrieved 0% by Random Attention, 0.4% SnapKV, 1.6% TriAttention, 34% VaSE, 84% R-KV, plausibly via R-KV's redundancy term favoring keys unlike the rest of the cache [Section 5.3, Table 3]. Open problems named: budgeting long prompts (code) and recovering once-stated facts [Section 1].

### Goal: What method assumption does its scoring or training rely on (per-token additive utility, per-head independence, teacher-forced or offline training)?
The paper's critique of prior work: all prior evictors score each cached token independently and keep the top-scoring ones, premised on the score deciding accuracy [Section 1]. Its own finding is that what decides retrieval is whether some usable copy survives in some head — a set-level property across heads and positions, not a per-token ranking [Section 5.2].


### Goal: How is the planted-fact probe built — what does "a head keeps the fact" mean, and which heads carry it?
A synthetic fact (fresh variable, 4-digit value, 16-token box) is forced token by token into real MATH500 reasoning used as filler, 1,536 tokens (15 eviction events) before a question; the model cannot restate it [full text, Section 5.2; Appendix B, Instrument]. A condition pins the fact span with +inf score in designated (layer, head) sites and −inf elsewhere; all other content gets per-head uniform eviction. Only three of Qwen3-4B's eight KV heads carry a usable trace alone; best single head 3%, the same two heads 60%, three 83%, all eight 99% (Fig 4a); two facts held in different heads give R = 0.31 together vs 0.10 and 0.16 alone (Fig 4b). Exact retrieval floors near 0.03 once a fact survives in only one head, and the probe runs only on Qwen3-4B (Appendix B, Caveats).

### Goal: Which redundancy (text restatement vs cross-head) carries real benchmark accuracy, and where does a selection signal still pay?
On real MATH500 traces a shared draw (same random positions in every head) is within 0.3 points of independent per-head draws at 4x and 8x, so "the text carries the load, and cross-head redundancy is a second line of defence for what the text does not restate" [Appendix H, Which redundancy does the work]. Text-level redundancy is inferred, not measured. A signal pays for facts stated once and needed much later (passcode: random 0%, R-KV 84%); the authors suggest, but did not evaluate, a hybrid that keeps the random draw for most of the budget and reserves a few slots per head for a content signal [Appendix H, Where a selection signal still pays].


### Goal: Is the cross-head superadditivity a threshold in the number of complete copies, or in the retained mass of the fact's tokens, and does it hold on graded recall as well as exact retrieval?
It is about retained mass, not complete copies. When the fact is dealt out token by token across heads, so that no head holds a readable span, retrieval is 0.33 against 0.39 for an intact sentence at the same retained mass, and graded recall is 0.75 vs 0.76 [full text, Section 5.2, "The shape of the copies does not matter"]. The 3% / 60% / 83% / 99% curve is exact-match retrieval for the best single head, "the same two heads", three heads and all eight (Fig 4a). The authors note exact retrieval "has no dynamic range once a fact survives in a single head (floors near 0.03)", which is why single-copy contrasts are reported on graded recall R [Appendix B, Caveats]. On R the two-fact cross-head effect is mild: 0.31 together against 0.10 and 0.16 alone (Fig 4b). So part of the sharp knee is the argmax readout of greedy decoding. Sites are (layer, head) pins; only three of eight KV heads are carriers, carrier identity is a property of the model, and the probe runs only on Qwen3-4B [Appendix B]. The authors read the result as "a value stays usable as long as some heads keep a copy, which is exactly what independent per-head draws maximise" [Section 5.2].
