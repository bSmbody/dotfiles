# R2 (generalist) review of C14 v3: "Keep the Attention Layers That Fix the Student's Own Mistakes"

## Summary
The paper converts Qwen3-4B (thinking) into a Gated DeltaNet hybrid and picks which 6 of 36 layers keep full attention with a greedy loop. Each round finds the first high-KL token on the student's own wrong long-CoT traces, keeps that position only if the teacher's continuations from it succeed clearly more often than the student's, and commits the layer or pair whose switch back to exact attention most often brings next-token KL under a tolerance. The resulting hybrid is shipped with Opal's on-policy distillation and placed on an accuracy-versus-growing-KV-bytes frontier against Opal's 9/36 layout under seven KV compressors. If every prediction lands exactly at its threshold, the paper shows four things. Ours-6 beats uniform-6 by 5 pp. It beats the same rounds with a random commit order by 5 pp. It sits at the 90th percentile of 16 random allocations and is within 3 pp of Opal-9 in bf16. At one dev-chosen extreme budget (B-dagger), ours-6 plus its best compressor beats Opal-9 plus its best compressor by 5 pp, and it may trail Opal by up to 3 pp at every other budget.

## Strengths
- The question matters to the venue. Memory that grows during long-CoT decoding is a real deployment cost, and converted hybrids (Opal [2609.31947], Apriel-H1, Super Apriel [2604.19877]) are a live topic at NeurIPS. Nobody has varied Opal's 9/36 ratio, and Opal's AIME gap to the teacher (52.5 vs 73.8) leaves room for allocation to matter [2609.31947].
- The one-line idea is memorable and easy to state: "keep the layers that repair the student's own mistakes, not the layers that look important on text the student never wrote." It follows the DAgger intuition and Opal's diagnosis that hybrids fail through compounding drift on their own states [2609.31947].
- The proposal treats bytes, not layer count, as the unit, and it is honest that FP8 on Opal-9 (4.5 L-eq) already beats six bf16 layers (6 L-eq). For a hybrid-allocation paper this is the right framing, and most papers in the area skip it.
- The experimental discipline is unusually good for this subfield. There is a week-1 inference-only kill test and a gate after short joint OPD. Choices are made on dev and the test set is evaluated once. The statistics are a hierarchical bootstrap over pipeline seeds with Holm correction. Five seeds on the headline arms, a 190-problem pool of recent competitions with AIME'24 held out for reproduction, a 3 pp reproduction tolerance on Opal, and uniform-6 trained through Opal's own recipe at matched GPU-hours. A reviewer cannot easily dismiss the headline numbers as noise.
- The rounds-only control (random commit order) directly answers the most obvious confound: LOOP found that iterating alone carried about 7 pp and its better score about 2 pp [2610.25679].

## Weaknesses
1. **[major] At threshold, the confirmed claims are against the weakest competitors. The comparisons that test the novelty are only point estimates.** The novelty is localizing failures to positions instead of using aggregate signals. The baselines that test this are greedy whole-trace on-policy KL, the LOOP-style on-policy score, on-policy gates, and DtR-style greedy dev accuracy, and the proposal predicts only a 3 pp point estimate over them, not significance. The Holm family is ours vs uniform-6, ours vs random commit order, and the composition test vs Opal. Uniform and random-order are exactly the baselines any reasonable selector beats; DtR [2601.11667] and LightTransfer [2410.13846] already show that data-driven selection beats fixed layouts. So a reader leaves with "on-policy selection beats uniform" (expected) and "localization might beat aggregate on-policy signals by 3 pp, not significant". The second statement is the paper's reason to exist.
2. **[major] The paper's own gate produces a cheap competitor that the method may only tie.** G1 trains 16 random 6-layer allocations with 250M-token joint OPD, about 4 shipping-run equivalents, and checks that the top quartile beats uniform-6 by at least 3 pp. At the stated threshold, ours sits at the 90th percentile of those 16 randoms, which is roughly the second-best random allocation. The obvious practitioner recipe is "run G1, pick the best on dev, ship it." That recipe costs less than the event miner, which is budgeted at up to 1.5 shipping runs plus seven mixer-only OPD rounds, and needs none of kappa, delta, tau, s_min, the sequential filter or the pair search. Best-of-16 is listed as a 3-seed baseline, but no prediction is stated against it. If ours does not clearly beat it, the method is a costly way to reach what random search plus a short training run already gives.
3. **[major] The memory headline is an accounting effect at one budget, and it mixes "six layers" with "our six layers."** C2 compares ours-6 with Opal-9, both compressed, at B-dagger. B-dagger is by construction the budget where Opal plus its best compressor has already lost at least 5 pp, probably at 1 or 0.5 L-eq (9-18x compression). At that budget six layers need about 1.5x less compression per layer than nine. BreadthKV [2610.05685] and the eviction benchmarks show that accuracy collapses steeply at extreme ratios, so a 5 pp win there is close to arithmetic. The frontier claim allows ours to trail Opal by up to 3 pp at every other budget, so the curves cross; this is not dominance. Most important, C2 is never run against uniform-6 plus a compressor. Without that comparison the reader cannot tell how much of the memory win comes from the selection method and how much from simply having fewer layers. LightTransfer already showed qualitatively that layer-level KV removal stacks with SnapKV [2410.13846].
4. **[major] The paper tells two stories, and neither is crisp alone.** The title has three clauses. The method has seven modules, two gates, a supernet, seven compressors, five budgets, about 14 baseline families and about 90 shipping-run equivalents. Read without the authors, the takeaway is "we built an elaborate pipeline and it beats uniform by 5 pp at one scale". An expert who has read the 15 or so hybrid layer-selection papers from the last year will see one more selection signal (KL-guided [2512.20569], FlashMorph [2606.30562], DASH [2605.20936], ConSA [2606.18056], HydraHead [2606.20097], NLL-guided [2606.27791], DtR [2601.11667], Super Apriel [2604.19877], LightTransfer [2410.13846], LOOP [2610.25679]). Its distinguishing word is "on-policy", and the experiments do not confirm that this word matters (point 1).
5. **[major] The evidence for the mechanism is weak at threshold.** The claim is that KL repair at a single failure token predicts whether the answer gets fixed. At threshold, committing the best candidate raises the 8-continuation success rate by 0.25 with Spearman 0.3 between KL reduction and success gain. That is a weak link. The selection is run in a teacher-anchored, mixer-only supernet, and frozen-vs-shipped rank agreement only has to reach Spearman 0.5. So the story "these layers fix these mistakes" passes through two loosely coupled proxies before it reaches the shipped model. Joint OPD changes MLPs and attention weights, and the shipped model may route around a missing layer, as SWARR found for RL [2606.11634]. The 5 pp gain could therefore come from a decent placement prior rather than from the stated mechanism.
6. **[minor] Scale and generality are narrow.** There is one primary 4B model plus MiMo-7B at 3 seeds, the work is math-only (LiveCodeBench must only stay within 2 pp of uniform), and the main result is a single point (K = 6) with K = 4 on the curve. The allocation is per checkpoint, so a practitioner has to rerun the whole loop for every model. The decode-throughput gain from 9 to 6 layers is stated to be below the 33% byte cut, so in serving terms the gain is small next to FP8, which costs nothing.
7. **[minor] Several compressors are adapted rather than used as published (GateSight decode-adapted), and memory for most compressors is computed, not measured.** The compressor is chosen per family on dev at each budget. That is fair, but it adds a second search on the dev pool, which favors whichever family has more knobs that fit the budget.
8. **[minor] Many free knobs.** kappa, delta, tau, s_min, events per round, the top-8 pair search, the placement sampler and the sequential filter leave room for implicit tuning on dev, even with pre-registration. The stability prediction (Jaccard ≥ 0.6 across the grid) at threshold means 4 of 6 layers are shared, so the "chosen layers" are only moderately stable.

## Overlapping prior and concurrent work
- **Opal [2609.31947]** is the base pipeline: the same models, Opal's 9/36 rule and the same OPD recipe. This work adds allocation on top. Opal never varies the ratio, so the gap the paper fills is real but narrow.
- **LightTransfer [2410.13846]** (TMLR 2025) is the closest older precedent for both halves of the story, and the proposal does not cite it. It converts a Transformer into a layer-wise hybrid by replacing "lazy" layers with streaming attention. On o1-like long reasoning (a 32B model, half the layers replaced, then SFT) its data-driven selection keeps AIME24 while LongGen's fixed layout loses 30% [full text, Section 6.2]. It beats Pyramid, random, Shapley and BERTology layouts [Section 6.3, Appendix C.5]. It reports that removing layers' KV beats SqueezeAttention at the same compression ratio and stacks with SnapKV [Section 6.3, Appendix C.2]. Its signal is teacher attention on fixed text, with no on-policy step, no iteration and no matched-bytes frontier against quantization, so it does not pre-empt the method. It does weaken the novelty of "data-driven selection beats rule-based layouts on long CoT" and "removing layers stacks with intra-layer compression".
- **LOOP [2610.25679]** (concurrent) uses the same loop structure: commit a layer, run short OPD, re-score from on-policy rollouts. The differences are that LOOP does depth pruning, uses aggregate per-layer scores and sets K by hand. The proposal's novelty over LOOP is localization plus restoration counting, and only the point-estimate comparison tests it (Weakness 1).
- **DtR [2601.11667]** runs greedy replacement by end-task validation score with a tolerance stop, and shows that static scores fail because layers interact. This is the "greedy, outcome-driven" ancestor. Its criterion is run here as a 3-seed baseline.
- **Super Apriel [2604.19877]** contributes the frozen-shared-weight supernet design and placement scoring on teacher-trace likelihood. The proposal reuses its design, so the supernet contributes nothing new.
- **KL-guided [2512.20569], FlashMorph [2606.30562], DASH [2605.20936], ConSA [2606.18056], HydraHead [2606.20097], NLL-guided [2606.27791]** are off-policy selectors, all cited.
- **MultiAlloc [2610.18642]** (concurrent, Oct 2026) frames KV compression as allocating a fixed byte budget across granularities (per-layer bit width vs per-token preservation) on reasoning models at matched budgets. It does not consider removing a layer's cache or hybrids, but the "spend the budget across granularities" framing behind C2 is already in the KV literature. BreadthKV [2610.05685] is the closest such result for long-CoT decode.
- **SWARR [2606.11634] and "Rethinking efficient attention in hybrids" [2606.15378]** are the main threat to significance. On-policy RL makes all-SWA nearly match full attention on math, and hybrid designs converge with enough training. The gates are designed to catch this, which is good.
- My recency searches (since 2025-10-29: hybrid attention, linear attention distillation, KV cache reasoning, hybrid layer allocation, on-policy distillation; and since 2026-10-15: hybrid attention layer, linearization, full attention layers) found no paper that allocates hybrid full-attention layers from localized on-policy failure positions. The specific combination appears novel.

## Missing baselines or experiments
- **Best-of-G1 random** (the 16 short-OPD random allocations, best picked on dev) as a confirmatory comparator at matched total GPU-hours, with a stated prediction. This is the cheapest serious alternative, and the pipeline already pays for it.
- **C2 against uniform-6 plus best compressor**, not only against Opal-9. Without it, the memory claim cannot be credited to the selection method.
- **Greedy whole-trace on-policy KL or DtR-style dev accuracy inside the Holm family**, in place of the random-commit-order control. This tests the "localization" claim directly. If power is the issue, drop the uniform comparison from the family instead, since beating uniform is not in doubt.
- **LightTransfer-style lazy-ratio selection** computed on student rollouts, as a cheap one-pass on-policy baseline.
- **A frontier including K = 9 under the selection method** (ours-9 vs Opal-9), to show whether the method helps at Opal's budget or only lets you go lower.
- **A from-scratch or natively trained hybrid reference** (e.g., a released GDN hybrid of similar size) on the same bytes axis, so readers can see where converted hybrids sit.
- **Wall-clock serving results under FP8 in vLLM** at realistic batch sizes, reporting measured gains from 9→6 layers, since the computed-bytes frontier overstates the practical effect.

## Questions for the authors
1. If ours-6 lands only at the 90th percentile of the 16 G1 random allocations, why would a practitioner run the event miner instead of picking the best G1 random allocation on dev? What is the measured GPU-hour cost of each route?
2. Does C2 hold for uniform-6 plus compressor vs Opal-9 plus compressor? If it does, which part of the memory claim is due to your selection?
3. Which budget do you expect B-dagger to be, and at that budget how much of ours-6's advantage disappears if Opal-9 may use per-layer non-uniform compression (MultiAlloc / KVTuner style) instead of one compressor setting for all nine layers?
4. How often do the six layers chosen in the frozen supernet stay among the top-6 by shipped accuracy after joint OPD? Spearman ≥ 0.5 over random allocations says little about the top of the ranking.
5. With a KL-repair to answer-repair correlation of 0.3, what share of the 5 pp gain over uniform would you attribute to repairing localized failures rather than to a generically better placement (e.g., middle-block layers, as in ConSA)?
6. Does the selected set transfer between Qwen3-4B and MiMo-7B by relative depth? If not, is the method per-checkpoint only?

## Rating
- rating: 3 (borderline reject)
- confidence: 4
- p_core: 0.3
- one_point_fix: Put the strongest cheap alternatives into the confirmatory Holm family in place of the uniform and random-order controls: best-of-16 G1 random allocations and greedy whole-trace on-policy KL, at matched total GPU-hours. Also rerun C2 against uniform-6 plus best compressor. A significant win over those would show that localizing failures on the student's own traces, rather than on-policy training or simply having fewer layers, earns the accuracy and the bytes.
