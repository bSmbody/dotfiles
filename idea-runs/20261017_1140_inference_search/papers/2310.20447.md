# Efficient Bayesian Learning Curve Extrapolation using Prior-Data Fitted Networks (LC-PFN)

## What this paper does
LC-PFN replaces MCMC in Bayesian learning-curve extrapolation with a prior-data fitted network: a transformer trained on 10M curves sampled from a parametric curve prior to output, in one forward pass, the posterior predictive distribution (PPD) of a run's later performance given its partial curve. It matches or beats MCMC on prior samples and on 20,000 real curves (LCBench, NAS-Bench-201, Taskset, PD1) while being >10,000x faster, and uses the PPD for a predictive early-stopping rule in model selection that gives 2-6x speed-ups on most tasks.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is the core mechanism (predicting the final value of a partial run with uncertainty), and why does it work?
Given a partial curve y_1..y_T and a prior p(y) over curves, approximate the PPD q(y_t' | y_1..y_T) for t' > T [full text, Section 3.1]. The prior is a weighted sum of three parametric growth curves plus Gaussian noise, restricted to [0,1] and to curves that end higher than they start [Section 3.2, Table 1]. A PFN is trained with cross-entropy to predict held-out curve values from random cutoffs of prior samples; minimising this loss minimises KL to the true PPD, so the network meta-learns Bayesian inference and applies it to real curves at test time [Section 3.3-3.4]. The output is a discretised distribution over 1,000 bins of equal prior mass [Section 3.4]. The motivation is that the dominant HPO practice, successive halving, rests on "the crude heuristic that learning curves mostly do not cross each other", while some curves are chaotic, so a method that quantifies how reliable its extrapolation is has an advantage [Section 1].

### Goal: How is the predictive early-stopping criterion defined, and what speed-up/regret does it achieve?
Following Domhan et al., a run is stopped when Pr(y_t' > y_best | y_1..y_T), the predicted chance that the run will beat the best model so far, does not exceed δ, with 1-δ = 0.95, applied either at 4 coarse cutoffs or at every epoch [full text, Section 4.3]. Over 2,120 model-selection experiments on 53 tasks, it beats no-stop and Patience(k) on every benchmark except NAS-Bench-201; the fine-grained variant reaches no-stop's regret about 3.3x faster on LCBench and Taskset, with 2-6x speed-ups on all PD1 and Taskset tasks and 30 of 35 LCBench tasks [Section 4.3 Results; Appendix C.3.1]. Aggressive Patience works on some tasks and fails on others, while the PPD-based rule is consistent across tasks [Appendix C.3.1]. Confidence levels 0.90-0.999 all work; higher levels give smaller but more consistent speed-ups [Appendix C.3.2]. Inference costs <100 ms per call [Section 5].

### Goal: What assumptions does the mechanism rely on, and where does it fail?
It relies on the prior covering the shapes of real curves. On NAS-Bench-201, whose curves have an inflection point "not resembling any in the prior", all termination criteria fail on all 3 tasks [full text, Section 4.3 Results; Appendix C.3.1]. Because it conditions on one partial curve only, it is "unaware of task hardness": applied after a single observation it can terminate runs on easy tasks, so a minimum of two observations is used [Appendix C.3.2]. The authors name conditioning on other curves of the same task (in-context meta-learning) and richer priors (divergence, slow start, heteroscedastic noise) as future work, and note that PFNs need a generative prior and may struggle with high-entropy priors [Section 5].


### Goal: Does LC-PFN's early-stopping evaluation compare against successive halving or Hyperband, and in what setting?
No. The baselines are no-stop (train every run to the full budget) and Patience(k) [full text, Section 4.3]. The setting is "vertical model selection where runs are considered in a fixed order and cannot be resumed" [Section 4.3, Fig 5]. The 2-6× speed-ups are measured "w.r.t. the final performance of no-stop" [Appendix C.3.1]. Successive halving appears only in the motivation, as "the crude heuristic that learning curves mostly do not cross each other" [Section 1]. Observations are available at every epoch, or at 4 coarse cutoffs in the coarse variant [Section 4.3].
