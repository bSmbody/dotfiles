# Helping or Herding? Reward Model Ensembles Mitigate but do not Eliminate Reward Hacking

## What this paper does
Eisenstein et al. train 25 T5 reward models (5 pretraining seeds × 5 fine-tuning seeds) on tl;dr, helpfulness and xsum/nli. They use them for best-of-n reranking and for RLHF. Reward models that agree in distribution disagree on aligned outputs (underspecification). Ensembles, especially ones whose members differ in pretraining seed, reduce overoptimisation. Some hacks survive ensembling because every member shares them.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: In best-of-n reranking, how much does the aggregation rule matter compared with ensemble diversity?
The aggregators are mean, median and mean_minus_std. Min was also tried but found "inferior to the alternatives" [full text, Section 4, "Building reward model ensembles"]. In best-of-n, "differences between aggregators are small, with mean usually performing at, or near, the top". The conservative ones (min, mean_minus_std) come out slightly ahead only at the smaller model scales on tl;dr [Section 4, "Evaluation"]. Diversity drives the gains. Pretrain ensembles beat finetune ensembles, which beat single reward models: at XL scale on tl;dr the win rates are 90%, 87.3% and 85.3% [Section 4, "Evaluation"; Fig 4]. Reward models from other pretraining seeds score the reranker's own top outputs lower. Reward models that share the reranker's pretraining seed overestimate them [Section 3, Fig 2].

### Goal: Which errors are shared across ensemble members, and why?
The paper tracks five qualitative hacks: list formatting, length, extractive copying, and dropping numbers (Fig 7 and Fig 10). Each is amplified by the ensemble and by its members alike, "because all members agree that these hacks improve the quality" [Section 5, Fig 7]. The explanation offered is that ensembles estimate uncertainty well near the decision boundary but underestimate it far from the training data. A policy can move into regions where "all reward models erroneously extrapolate in the same manner" [Section 5]. In addition, fine-tuned autoevaluators trained on the same preference data can overstate performance [Section 4]. All experiments are on LLM text.
