# In-Context Freeze-Thaw Bayesian Optimization for Hyperparameter Optimization (ifBO / FT-PFN)

## What this paper does
ifBO is a freeze-thaw HPO method: training runs are advanced one step at a time, and at each step a surrogate predicts every configuration's later performance from all partial learning curves observed so far, and an acquisition function decides which run to continue (or which new run to start), while the others are paused and can be resumed later. Its surrogate, FT-PFN, is a prior-data fitted network trained only on synthetic curve collections, which infers the posterior predictive in context in one forward pass without online refitting. With the randomized MFPI acquisition it is state of the art or competitive on LCBench, Taskset and PD1 in the low-budget regime.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: How does FT-PFN condition on the partial curves of all configurations (in context), and what does that add over single-curve extrapolation?
Each meta-training example is a collection of partial curves of many configurations on one task, with targets at later budgets of those same configurations [full text, Section 4.1; Appendix A.2]. The prior encodes task-level structure: curves "on the same task are expected to have similar start, saturation, and convergence points", and y_0 and y_max are shared across configurations of a task [Section 4.1 "Prior desiderata"; Appendix A.2]. Every observed (configuration, step, value) is a token, attention runs over all observed tokens, and the output is a 1,000-bin PPD for any query [Appendix A.3]. Thus each run's prediction borrows strength from its siblings. A "no HPs" variant that sees only the set of partial curves also beats the DyHPO and DPL surrogates in log-likelihood [Section 5.1, Table 1]. The curve prior also allows breaking points and divergence, which improves both prediction and HPO [Appendix D.1].

### Goal: How is the continue/start/pause decision made from the predictive distribution, and why does it beat discard-based multi-fidelity methods (Hyperband/successive halving)?
At each step the run maximising MFPI(λ; h, T) = P(M(λ, b_λ+h) > T) is continued, i.e. the predicted chance of exceeding a threshold T ≥ f_best after h more steps; h and T are re-randomised every step (log10 τ ~ U(-4,-1)), which hedges over a portfolio of PI criteria [full text, Section 4.2, Eq 3-4]. Runs not chosen are frozen, not discarded. Hyperband-style methods "do not allow the continuation of previously discarded runs in the light of new evidence" and depend on "a strong correlation between the ranks ... at low and high fidelities (e.g., no crossings of learning curves)" [Section 2]. Freeze-thaw methods (ifBO, DyHPO, DPL) beat Hyperband, ASHA and random search in the low-budget regime [Section 5.2, Fig 3]. EI-based acquisitions with FT-PFN fail, because its justified uncertainty gives heavy-tailed posteriors that drive over-exploration [Section 5.3].

### Goal: What evidence is there that the predictive uncertainty is calibrated and cheap, and what are its limits?
FT-PFN has much higher log-likelihood than DPL (overconfident) and DyHPO (one-step extrapolation only), and lower or comparable MSE, at 10-100x lower runtime, including with >1,000 context points [full text, Section 5.1, Table 1]. One model trained in ~8 GPU-hours on an RTX 2080 serves all experiments without fine-tuning [Appendix A.3]. Limits: metrics must be normalised to [0,1], budgets ≤ 1,000 steps, ≤ 10 hyperparameters, and the prior is synthetic and generic [Section 6; Appendix A.2 "Limitations"].
