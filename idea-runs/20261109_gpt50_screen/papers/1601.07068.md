# Analyzing the effect of local rounding error propagation on the maximal attainable accuracy of the pipelined Conjugate Gradient method

## What this paper does
Pipelined CG hides global-reduction latency by replacing explicit computations (e.g., the residual b - Ax) with cheap extra recurrences that update auxiliary vectors incrementally. In finite precision those recurrences accumulate local rounding errors, so the recursively updated residual drifts away from the true residual and the attainable accuracy stagnates orders of magnitude above classical CG. The paper bounds this residual gap, gives a cheap running estimate of it, and proposes automated residual replacement: at selected iterations the drifting recursive quantities are recomputed explicitly from ground truth (b, A and the current iterate), which resets the gap. In PETSc on 20 nodes the replacement version keeps almost all of the pipelined speedup (3.4x vs 3.8x over classical CG).

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: What is the core mechanism of residual replacement and why does it work?
Pipelined CG computes r, u, w, s, q, z by recurrences instead of explicit sparse matrix-vector products; in exact arithmetic these are equivalent, but each recurrence injects local rounding errors that propagate and are amplified much more than in classical CG [full text, Section 1; Section 2.3]. Residual replacement, at chosen iterations, recomputes those vectors explicitly from their definitions (r = b - A x, u = M^-1 r, w = A u, s = A p, ...) [Section 3, Eq 57]. Only quantities with an explicit formula are replaced; the iterate x and search direction p are not, "since no explicit formulae for these vectors are available" [Section 3]. Replacement eliminates the accumulated gap f between the true and recursive residual [Section 3], and experiments show attainable accuracy improves on most problems where plain pipelined CG stagnates [Section 4.2, Fig 3; Section 5].

### Goal: When does it replace, and what does a replacement cost?
Computing the true residual every iteration "is computationally much too expensive" [Section 2.4], so a cheap running estimate of the gap (a linear recurrence on norms of local error bounds, Eq 54) is tracked and replacement fires only when the estimated gap crosses a threshold tau = sqrt(eps) relative to the residual norm, and only once per crossing, Eq 61 [Section 3]. Replacing too often or when the residual is small can delay convergence [Section 3; Section 5]. Cost is the extra spmvs at replacement steps; e.g., 39 replacements over 1474 iterations, and parallel speedup over CG drops only from 3.8x to 3.4x [Section 4.3].

### Goal: What assumption does the mechanism rely on, and what does it not fix?
It assumes the Krylov basis has not lost orthogonality critically; the authors call the strategy a heuristic rather than a theoretically validated fix [Section 3; Section 5]. It does not repair convergence delay from loss of orthogonality, and the replacement itself can introduce errors and delay convergence on some problems (e.g., nos1) [Section 1; Section 4.2; Section 5].
