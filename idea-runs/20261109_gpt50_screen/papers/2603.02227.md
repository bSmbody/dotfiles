# Routing Absorption in Sparse Attention: Why Random Gates Are Hard to Beat

## What this paper does
Controlled study (31M transformer on WikiText-103, plus Qwen3-1.7B partial fine-tuning) of learned per-query token-level gates for sparse attention. Learned gates trained jointly with the model barely beat frozen random gates, which the authors call routing absorption: Q/K/V co-adapt to whatever mask is imposed. Gates distilled post-hoc onto frozen dense Q/K/V reach near-oracle masks quickly.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: Does a hard top-k sparse attention gate receive gradient from the task loss, and what happens with soft gates?
No: with mask M = 1[rank(G) <= k], dM/dG = 0, and the topk -> scatter -> masked_fill chain gives no gradient path; learned and frozen random gates give 71.22 vs 71.24 ppl [full text, Section 3.2]. Differentiable soft gating restores a gradient but only yields 48.73 vs 49.83 ppl for learned vs random gates; after 50K steps removing or randomizing the gate barely matters [Section 1, Section 3.1].

### Goal: What does it conclude about how routing should be trained, and what are its limits?
A bilinear gate trained post-hoc on a frozen dense checkpoint closes >94% of the random-to-oracle gap in ~1000 steps; gates distilled onto co-adapted Q/K/V reach high F1 but hard-mask deployment gives 601.6 ppl vs 48.6 for dense-trained Q/K/V [Section 1, Section 3.3]. It recommends decoupling routing from model adaptation and random-routing controls [Section 6.1, 6.3]. Limits: absorption is shown for per-query token-level gates; NSA's block-level selection with a compression branch may differ; full sparse pretraining at 1.7B is out of scope [Section 6.1, Section 7].
