# Retrieval Head Mechanistically Explains Long-Context Factuality

## What this paper does
Identifies "retrieval heads": a sparse (<5%), universal and intrinsic set of attention heads that copy tokens from the context to the output during decoding. It defines a retrieval score from copy-paste events in needle-in-a-haystack runs. Masking these heads breaks needle retrieval, extractive QA and chain-of-thought reasoning, and the authors suggest using this for KV cache compression.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: How is a copy (retrieval) event detected at decoding time, and how are retrieval heads identified?
At each decoding step, with generated token w and head attention a, head h copy-pastes if (1) w is a needle token and (2) the argmax-attended input position j holds the same token as w and lies in the needle [full text, Section 2]. The retrieval score is the fraction of needle tokens a head copy-pasted (Eq 1). It is averaged over about 600 NIAH instances (3 needle sets × 20 lengths 1K-50K × 10 depths), and heads above 0.1 are retrieval heads [Section 2].

### Goal: Are retrieval heads sparse, stable and context-dependent?
Fewer than 5% of heads score above 0.5 in all models studied [Section 3.1, Fig 3]. Some heads (12 in Llama-2-7B-80K, 36 in Yi-6B-200K) are always activated. Weaker heads fire only on certain tokens or contexts [Section 3.2]. The same heads persist through length extension, SFT and RLHF [Section 3.3].

### Goal: Do retrieval heads matter for chain-of-thought, and what does the paper say about KV compression?
Masking retrieval heads significantly hurts CoT on MMLU, MuSiQue and GSM8K but not answer-only prompting, because "in a reasoning chain, the next step reasoning requires the model to refer to previous information"; the model becomes "blind" and hallucinates [Section 4.3, Fig 10-11]. The authors suggest pruning the KV of non-retrieval heads, but leave this to future work [Section 5]. The detector uses a literal-copy criterion (same token attended and emitted).
