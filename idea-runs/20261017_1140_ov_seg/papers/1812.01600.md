# AutoFocus: Efficient Multi-Scale Inference

## What this paper does
AutoFocus (ICCV 2019) speeds up multi-scale object detection by running the detector coarse-to-fine: at each scale a learned head predicts category-agnostic "FocusPixels" (feature-map cells overlapping small objects), these are dilated and enclosed in rectangular "FocusChips", and only the chips are upsampled and processed at the next scale. Detections from all scales are merged after pruning chip-boundary detections. On COCO it matches a full multi-scale pyramid while processing 2.8x fewer pixels, and loses 1% mAP at 5x fewer.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: How does AutoFocus decide where to process at a finer scale, and what supervision does that decision need?
A FocusPixel is a conv5 cell overlapping a ground-truth box whose scaled side lies between a = 5 and b = 64 px; cells of smaller boxes or of boxes between 64 and 90 px are ignored, the rest are negatives [full text, Sec 4.1, label equation]. Two conv layers and a binary softmax on top of conv5 are trained to predict this map, so the "where to zoom" decision is a supervised, class-agnostic small-object detector trained on box annotations [Sec 4.1]. At inference, pixels above a threshold t are dilated (d x d), grouped into connected components, enclosed in chips of minimum size k, and overlapping chips merged (Alg 1) [Sec 4.2].

### Goal: What assumption does the coarse-to-fine scheme rely on, and what evidence supports it?
It assumes small objects can be localized (not necessarily recognized) at a coarser scale with high recall and that they occupy a small area: 40% of COCO instances are small but cover only 0.3% of image area [Sec 1, Fig 1]; FocusPixels are about 4% of pixels at scale 3 and 11% at scale 2 [Sec 5.1]. Predicted FocusPixels covering 5% of the image at scale 2 already include all confidently detected objects; at scale 1 they need 25% [Sec 5.2]. Context matters: shrinking minimum chip size raises speed-up but "a reasonable amount of context is necessary" [Sec 5.1].

### Goal: What errors arise when results from zoomed chips are merged, and how are they handled?
Cropping can cut large objects so that their parts look small at the next scale and produce false positives (e.g. a horse rider at the chip border). Dilation ensures true small objects lie inside chips, so detections touching a chip boundary (not shared with the image boundary) are discarded; valid detections are projected back and merged with NMS [Sec 4.3, Fig 4].
