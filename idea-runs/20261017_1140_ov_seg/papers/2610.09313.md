# Why VLMs Miss Small Objects, and When Zooming In Is Safe

## What this paper does
A concurrent (2026-10) theory-plus-measurement paper on why vision-language models miss small objects in large images. It describes every call by S, the number of visual tokens across an object's side, and L, the content a call must cover. From the interface alone it bounds the S a whole image can reach and the token cost of covering an image at a given S. Under monotonicity assumptions (more tokens per target and less content per call do not hurt), it proves when splitting an image into crops cannot lower recall, extends this to pixel-labelling tasks, and shows that adaptive zooming obeys a recall–cost frontier. About 177,000 requests on 797 images (drawings, floor plans, FSC-147) with eleven VLMs test it.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: (innovator C2) Under what condition does splitting an image into zoomed crops provably not hurt, and does it cover per-pixel labelling (segmentation)?
Theorem 1: if every crop has S at least that of the whole image ("no view zooms out") and the views overlap so each target lies inside some view with half a target box of margin, then under (R) and (M) the crops together detect at least as many targets as the whole-image call, in expectation [full text, Sec 3.2, Thm 1]. Proposition 4 shows the condition is exact: for a target that lies in no view's kept core at S ≥ S_w, a model satisfying all assumptions can exist under which the whole image finds it and the decomposition never does; and a recipe that rescales images so targets span a fixed pixel size, then tiles, "zooms out" on large targets and loses the guarantee [Sec 3.2, Prop 4]. Section 3.3 extends this to assignment tasks, explicitly including labelling each pixel: an item has a support box "containing the pixels needed to answer the item", and Theorem 4 says a decomposition whose crops partition the image, each view containing its core enlarged by the support box, has at least the whole image's expected number of correct answers [Sec 3.3, Thm 4]. So for segmentation the guarantee holds only for items whose needed support lies inside the view; nothing is guaranteed for pixels whose correct label needs context beyond the crop. Empirically, a 44 px tile recipe that zooms out on large symbols lowered recall in 19 of 21 cells [Sec 1, Sec 5.1].

### Goal: (innovator C2) Can an adaptive zoom policy beat uniform tiling in cost, and what must hold for that?
Proposition 8: under a hard recognition threshold S_rec, any adaptive policy that is always right must spend at least about the coverage cost of tiling the admissible area at S_rec; zoom agents "do not escape decomposition; they perform it" and save only area excluded a priori [Sec 3.5, Prop 8]. Proposition 9 replaces the threshold by a graded recognition curve f(S): the cost lower bound scales with 1/κ_f, κ_f = sup f(S)/S². If f rises like S² or faster at small S, every search pays the coverage cost; "if recognition rose more slowly than S² at small S, the bound would be vacuous, and spreading tokens thinly over the whole image could pay" [Sec 3.5, Prop 9]. So adaptive zoom can save cost only if coarse views carry graded evidence about sub-token targets.

### Goal: (innovator C2) What do they measure about recognition below one token per object side?
Recall rises with S "gradually rather than at a sharp threshold" for every model in synthetic sweeps. Half-recall is reached at S = 0.31 (Gemma 4), 0.35 (Claude Sonnet 4.6), 0.54 (Qwen3-VL) and at or below 0.13–0.29 for GPT-5.4/5.6, Gemini 3.8 and Claude Sonnet 5.5; only Gemini 2.5 needs about one token (S_50 = 1.28) [Sec 5.2]. Within one call, recall also falls as content grows at fixed S (e.g. −0.22 F1 for GPT-5.6 when eight blocks surround the target block) [Sec 5.3]. Monotonicity failed only for OpenAI high-resolution paths [Sec 5.1].
