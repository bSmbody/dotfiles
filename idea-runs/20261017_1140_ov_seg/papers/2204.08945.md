# Missingness Bias in Model Debugging

## What this paper does
Jain et al. (ICLR 2022) study what happens when image regions are "removed" from a classifier's input, as model-debugging tools such as LIME do. Pixel-level stand-ins for missing content (black, mean or random colour, noise, blur) make CNN predictions depend on the masking pattern itself rather than on the remaining content, a "missingness bias". Because a ViT processes a set of positionally encoded tokens, the removed region's tokens can instead be dropped from the forward pass. Across architectures, fill types, patch sizes and superpixels, dropping tokens largely removes the bias without retraining, while CNNs need retraining with masking augmentation.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: (innovator C3) When a region of the input is masked out with a constant or blurred fill, does the model's prediction come from the remaining content or from the mask itself, and does this depend on architecture?
For a ResNet-50 with blacked-out 16×16 patches, predictions skew toward a few unrelated classes (maze, crossword puzzle, carton) and class entropy collapses; it "rapidly flips its predictions even when the less relevant regions are removed first", and by 25% masked area its predictions become largely irrelevant to the image [full text, Sec 2.1, Sec 3, Figs 2–6]. The model "seems to be relying on the masking pattern to make the prediction, rather than the remaining (unmasked) portions" [Sec 2.1]. Mean-colour, random-colour and per-pixel random fills behave the same [App C.4, Fig 17]. Blur reduces the bias but "can still leak information from the removed regions" [App C.4, Fig 18]. A ViT-S that blacks out pixels is already much less biased than the ResNet, though less robust than dropping tokens [App C.7, Fig 21].

### Goal: (innovator C3) How can a vision transformer remove a region without a fill, and does it keep using the rest of the image?
After tokenisation, the tokens of the removed region are dropped and the rest keep their original positional encodings; batches with different lengths use dummy tokens with masked self-attention [App B]. A region that does not align with the token grid is removed conservatively by dropping every token it touches [App B]. With dropped tokens the ViT keeps a near-uniform class distribution, keeps its original prediction even when large parts of the image are removed, and when the prediction changes it predicts WordNet-related classes, i.e. it "successfully leverage[s] the remaining features" [Sec 2.2, Sec 3, Figs 3–6]. Retraining with missingness augmentation is unnecessary for the ViT [Sec 3, Fig 5]. Results are for ImageNet classifiers, not for detectors or segmenters.


### Goal: (reviewer C3) Does the paper test whether dropping the tokens that carry a class's evidence makes the model report that class as absent, and is it shown for detectors, segmenters or windowed-attention backbones?
No on both counts. Patches are removed in random, most-salient-first and least-salient-first orders, and the paper measures the entropy of the output class distribution, the fraction of predictions that change, and WordNet similarity between old and new predictions [full text, Sec 3, App C.2, Fig 12]. These measure freedom from bias toward the hole, not a per-class "still present?" decision: the ViT "maintains its original predictions even when large parts of the image are removed" and, when its prediction does change, still predicts classes related to the original [Sec 3, Figs 4–6]. The models are ImageNet classifiers only: ViT-T and ViT-S with global self-attention, against ResNet-18/50 (plus other CNNs in App C.3) [App A.1, Table 1]. Token dropping is implemented by removing tokens before the self-attention layers and masking attention for padded batches [App B]; windowed attention, convolutional necks, detectors and segmenters are not covered.
