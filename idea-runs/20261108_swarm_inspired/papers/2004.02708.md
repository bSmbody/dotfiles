# Sequential adaptive strategy for population-based sampling of a rare and clustered disease

## What this paper does
Proposes Poisson Sequential Adaptive (PoSA) sampling and a size-controlled variant (CPoSA) for surveys of a rare, spatially clustered trait (tuberculosis prevalence). Units are visited in a pre-chosen one-dimensional order; each is included by an independent Bernoulli draw, except that a detected positive forces inclusion of the next unit. Because every inclusion probability is tracked in a Bondesson-Thornburn updating matrix, a Horvitz-Thompson estimator stays exactly unbiased, with closed-form variance. Simulations compare against the WHO multi-stage cross-sectional design.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: How does PoSA adapt sampling to detected positives while keeping estimates unbiased?
Units are ordered so that neighbors are adjacent in the list. Unit i is drawn with probability pi_i^(i-1): set to 1 if the previous unit was sampled and positive, else its initial pi_i^(0) [full text, Section 3, Eq 3-4]. Only consecutive pairs become dependent, so joint inclusion probabilities and covariances are closed-form [Eq 7-8]. The HT estimator sum_{i in s} y_i / pi_i^(i-1) is unbiased, with an exact unbiased variance estimator [Eq 9-11]. Over-sampling positives "purposely introduce[s] a selection bias", which the weights remove at estimation time [Section 2].

### Goal: How is the sampling budget controlled, and when does the adaptive design help or hurt?
CPoSA fixes a minimum size by choosing initial probabilities that sum to n_min, then adjusts later probabilities by (s_i - pi_i)/(N-i) so that extra draws are spent only on adaptively found positives [Section 4, Eq 12]. A hard maximum size is left as future work because truncation complicates unbiasedness [Section 6]. In simulation the between-area variability k (the degree of clustering) drives the comparison: detection rate and cost per detected case improve uniformly as k grows. The price is a larger final sample (up to 1.5x) and lower accuracy of the prevalence estimate itself [Section 5, Figures 2-3]. So the design is best for finding positives cheaply, not for the most precise mean.
