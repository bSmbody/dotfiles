# AMReX: Block-Structured Adaptive Mesh Refinement for Multiphysics Applications

## What this paper does
Describes AMReX, the exascale software framework for block-structured adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) used by ECP applications (astrophysics, combustion, cosmology, accelerators, multiphase flow). The solution lives on a hierarchy of levels: a coarse level covering the whole domain and finer levels made of disjoint rectangular patches covering only regions that need resolution. The paper covers data containers and iterators, regridding and load balancing, inter-level operations (coarse-to-fine interpolation, fine-to-coarse restriction, refluxing), particles, embedded boundaries and GPU performance portability. Main claim: AMR reduces compute and memory relative to a uniform fine mesh while preserving accuracy where it matters.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: How does block-structured AMR decide where to refine, and how often?
Regridding creates or modifies grids at level l+1 "at user-specified intervals". A user-defined error estimate tags individual cells at level l above a tolerance (typical criteria: first or second derivatives of state variables, or an application-specific heuristic). Tagged cells are clustered into rectangular patches (Berger-Rigoutsos), which are refined to form level l+1, repeated until the error criterion is met or the maximum level is reached [full text, Section 5.2]. Only the coarsest level must cover the domain; finer levels are properly nested [Section 2]. In the Rayleigh-Taylor benchmark regridding is done every 2 steps on each fine level [Section 7, performance test].
### Goal: How is a new fine patch filled, and how are fine and coarse levels kept consistent?
Three inter-level operations [Section 6.4]: (1) interpolation from coarse to fine, used to fill fine ghost cells that cannot be copied from a same-level patch and, during regridding, to fill fine regions not previously covered by fine patches; coarse data are fetched only "when fine data are not available"; (2) restriction, which synchronizes levels by replacing coarse data with an average or injection of fine data; (3) refluxing, which corrects coarse fluxes at coarse/fine boundaries with time- and space-averaged fine fluxes. Ghost cells around a patch are filled by interpolating from coarser data, copying from neighbours, or physical boundary conditions [Section 6.1]. So a fine patch is advanced locally, with its boundary taken from the coarse level, and the coarse level is then overwritten by the fine result.
### Goal: What share of the domain is refined in practice?
In the reported Rayleigh-Taylor runs, levels 1 and 2 initially occupy 12.5% and 6.25% of the domain [Section 7]. The paper gives no general statement beyond the claim that AMR cuts cost and memory footprint relative to a uniform mesh [abstract].
