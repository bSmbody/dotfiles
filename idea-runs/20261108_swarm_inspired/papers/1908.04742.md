# Online Continual Learning with Maximally Interfered Retrieval

## What this paper does
In online continual learning with a small replay memory (or a generative model), MIR picks which stored samples to replay by a virtual update: take the would-be SGD step on the incoming batch, then choose the stored samples whose loss would rise most under it. It beats random replay in accuracy and forgetting on MNIST Split, Permuted MNIST and CIFAR-10, and the gap grows with memory size.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is MIR's core criterion for which stored samples matter, and why does it work?
For incoming (X_t, Y_t), compute theta^v = theta - alpha grad L(f_theta(X_t), Y_t), then rank memory samples by s_MI-1(x) = l(f_{theta^v}(x), y) - l(f_theta(x), y), or s_MI-2, which subtracts the best loss seen so far [full text, Section 3.1]. The motivation is that "the loss of some previous samples may be unaffected or even improved, thus retraining on them is wasteful" [Section 3]. The memory spends its effort on what the next update will damage, not on everything stored. A random pre-subsample of C > B candidates keeps the search cheap and diverse [Section 3.1].

### Goal: What does it rely on, and how large are the gains?
It relies on the foreseen update being estimable before it happens (one virtual gradient step on the incoming batch) and on interference being predictable from that step [Section 3.1]. ER-MIR improves accuracy and forgetting over ER on MNIST variants and CIFAR-10, with larger gaps at larger memories [Section 4.1]. On MNIST Split, ER-MIR reaches 87.6 accuracy and 7.0 forgetting against ER's 82.1 and 15.0, and GEN-MIR reaches 82.1 against GEN's 79.3 [Section 4.1, Table 1].
