# Improving Video Generation with Human Feedback

## What this paper does
The paper builds a large human-preference dataset: 182k prompt–video–video triplets from 12 modern T2V models, each labelled pairwise on Visual Quality (VQ), Motion Quality (MQ) and Text Alignment (TA). On this data it trains VideoReward, a Qwen2-VL-2B reward model fitted with a Bradley–Terry-with-ties loss, as part of the VideoAlign pipeline. It then derives three alignment methods for rectified-flow video models: Flow-DPO and Flow-RWR (training-time) and Flow-NRG (inference-time reward guidance on noisy latents). Finally, it releases VideoGen-RewardBench, 26.5k annotated pairs from modern T2V models.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What does the VideoReward/VideoAlign model score, how is it built, and how reliable is it as an automatic judge of motion quality and text alignment?
- **Labels.** Annotators recorded pairwise A wins / tie / B wins separately for VQ, MQ and TA. They also gave 1–5 Likert scores [full text, Section 3.1].
- **Model.** The backbone is Qwen2-VL-2B. Two context-agnostic tokens, [VQ] and [MQ], are placed after the video and before the prompt, so they see only visual content. A context-aware [TA] token goes after the prompt. A shared linear head maps the three tokens to scores [Section 3.2, "Token Positioning"]. Training uses a BT-with-ties loss with θ = 5 [Eq 1–2].
- **Benchmark result.** It beats VideoScore, LiFT and VisionReward on VideoGen-RewardBench pairwise accuracy for VQ, MQ and TA, and is comparable on GenAI-Bench [Section 5.1, Table 2].
- **Limitations.** The authors say the model "still carries a potential risk of reward hacking", where human ratings drop while the reward stays high [Section 7].

For an evaluation study, MQ and TA are therefore usable as automatic per-video preference proxies for motion quality and alignment. They should be backed by a human check rather than optimised against.
