# Learning-based Video Motion Magnification

## What this paper does
Eulerian video motion magnification with learned filters (ECCV 2018). A CNN encoder splits each frame into a "shape" representation meant to vary linearly with displacement and a separate "texture" (intensity) representation; a manipulator multiplies the difference of shape representations between two frames by a magnification factor, and a decoder rebuilds the magnified frame. Trained on synthetic two-frame pairs with sub-pixel motion, it can also be run with classical linear temporal band-pass filters over the per-frame shape representation. It reports fewer ringing artifacts and better noise behaviour than phase-based Eulerian magnification, and degrades at high magnification with temporal filtering.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: What exactly does Eulerian magnification amplify, and how is the amplification written as an operation on a difference between frames?
Motion magnification is defined on a video I(x,t) = f(x + δ(x,t)): the target is f(x + (1+α)δ(x,t)), and in practice only a selected part T(δ) of the motion is magnified, "typically a temporal bandpass filter" [full text, Section 3.1 / Eq 1]. Eulerian methods do this without tracking: decompose frames into a representation, manipulate it, reconstruct [Section 2]. The manipulator is a linear extrapolation of the difference between two frames' shape representations, Ma + α(Mb − Ma) [Section 3.2 / Eq 2]; with a temporal filter it becomes M(t) + αT(M(t)) [Section 3.2 / Eq 4]. Two reference choices exist: "Static mode" magnifies displacement against the first frame; "Dynamic mode" uses the previous frame and so magnifies velocity between consecutive frames, which handles larger motion [Section 3.2, "Applying 2-frames setting to videos"; Fig. 1].

### Goal: Why must the frame-constant (static/intensity) part be kept out of the amplified signal, and what goes wrong if it is not?
Eq 1 assumes constant intensity f, but real frames also change intensity, which "causes our network to also magnify intensity changes". The fix is a separate texture representation (analogous to steerable-pyramid amplitude) that is not magnified; this "reduces undesired intensity magnification as well as noise in the final output" [Section 3.2]. Training adds regularisers so that colour perturbations change texture but not shape [Section 3.2, Loss function]. So the magnified channel is restricted to the part that encodes position, and appearance is passed through unmagnified.

### Goal: What are the failure modes of linear amplification (noise, blur, large factors) and what role does temporal band selection play?
The temporal filter "helps to isolate motions of interest and to prevent noise from being magnified" [Section 2]. Without it, "undesired motion and noise quickly becomes a problem as the magnification factor increases" [Section 3.2]. A purely linear manipulator "does blur strong edges ... and is more prone to noise" (Fig. 3) [Section 3.2]. With temporal filters the learned representation is "linear enough" only "up to a moderate magnification factor"; quality degrades beyond about 20× (Fig. 11), and very small motions that need a temporal filter can be missed (Fig. 12) [Section 4.4]. Classical band-pass Eulerian methods degrade when "large motions or drifts occur" [Section 2].


### Goal: Which Eulerian variant does a plain additive temporal decomposition of intensities (no position-encoding representation) correspond to, and what limits does the paper state for it?
The paper separates Eulerian methods by their representation. Wu et al. [30] "use a spatial decomposition motivated by the first-order Taylor expansion", i.e. they magnify temporally band-passed intensity variation. Wadhwa et al. use the phase of a complex steerable pyramid, and this paper learns a shape representation [full text, Section 2]. The hand-designed Eulerian methods "are prone to noise and often suffer from excessive blurring". The band-pass methods of [30] and [26, 27] suffer "degraded quality when large motions or drifts occur in the input video"; handling large motion needed affine motion models or acceleration-based temporal processing [Section 2]. The paper's own fix for intensity change is to keep a separate texture representation out of the magnifier [Section 3.2]. Even its learned linear manipulator "does blur strong edges ... and is more prone to noise" than a non-linear one [Section 3.2, Fig. 3]. Wu et al.'s SIGGRAPH 2012 paper is not on arXiv and was not read; these statements are this paper's characterisation of it.
