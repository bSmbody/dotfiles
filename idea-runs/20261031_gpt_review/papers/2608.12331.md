# Thought-Aware KV Cache Compaction for Reasoning via Adaptive Attention Matching

## What this paper does
TAM adds structure to Attention Matching compaction for long reasoning traces on Qwen3-4B (AIME 2024, MATH-500). It splits the trace into reasoning segments at double newlines, gives each segment a share of the compact-key budget proportional to sqrt(importance × size), where importance is the attention the segment receives from a recent selector window, and keeps "pivotal" tokens whose attention exceeds three times the mean uncompressed. It re-runs this every P generated tokens.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: How is segment importance defined and how is the budget allocated?
Importance w_i is the mean attention mass that segment S_i receives from the last R query positions [full text, Section 4.4, Eq 5]. After reserving pivotal tokens, segment budgets are t_i ∝ sqrt(w_i n_i) (Eq 6), optimal for a convex per-segment error under a linear error model (Proposition C.1). Pivotal tokens are those with mean selector-window attention above c × mean, c = 3 [Section 4.5, Eq 7]; the authors call them "problem constants, key intermediate results, critical definitions".

### Goal: Is the retention decision set-level, and does it model which segments depend on or restate which?
No. Each segment's value is its own current attention from the frontier, and the error model is a weighted sum of independent per-segment errors [Section 4.4; Appendix C, Remark C.4 "Linear Composition Assumption"]. Nothing models dependencies between segments, restated copies, or whether a value could be recovered from what survives. Pivotal tokens get no memory across rounds: "they compete for retention based on their current attention scores" [Section 4.6].

### Goal: What limitations does it state?
Heuristic segmentation and a local selector window "can fail ... when long-range dependencies and backtracking occur"; evaluation is on math benchmarks and one model, Qwen3-4B [Limitations].
