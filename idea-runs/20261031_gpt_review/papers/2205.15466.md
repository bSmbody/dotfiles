# Data Banzhaf: A Robust Data Valuation Framework for Machine Learning

## What this paper does
Studies how to value each training point when the utility (model performance on a subset) is noisy because training is stochastic. Leave-one-out (LOO) and Shapley values produce rankings that flip from run to run. All semivalues are weighted averages of a point's marginal contribution over subsets; the paper defines a safety margin (the smallest utility perturbation that can reverse a distinguishable pair) and proves the Banzhaf value, which weights all subsets uniformly, has the largest margin. It gives the Maximum Sample Reuse (MSR) estimator, in which every sampled subset's utility is used for every point, saving a factor n in sample complexity.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: Why does leave-one-out underperform, and how do semivalues generalize it?
LOO is U(N) − U(N \ i), the marginal contribution only against the full set [full text, Section 2, Eq 1]. Every semivalue is a weighted average of marginal contributions U(S ∪ i) − U(S) over subsets S, with LOO as the weight concentrated at |S| = n−1 and Shapley as uniform-over-sizes [Section 2, Theorem 2.1, Eq 2]. Empirically, the variance of LOO and Shapley estimates under SGD noise "significantly outweighs their magnitude" and fewer than 10% of points stay consistently in the top/bottom k% [Section 3, Figures 1-2].

### Goal: What makes a value notion robust to noisy utilities, and which one is most robust?
Safety margin = the minimum perturbation ||Û − U|| that reverses the order of a pair that U distinguishes by τ on every subset size [Section 4.1, Definitions 4.1-4.2]. LOO has margin τ, the smallest; Shapley is larger; Banzhaf, phi(i) = E_{S ~ Unif(2^{N\i})}[U(S ∪ i) − U(S)], attains the largest, τ 2^{n/2−1}, among all semivalues [Theorems 4.4, 4.6]. Intuition: putting all weight on a few subsets lets a perturbation of those subsets move the value; uniform weighting spreads it [Section 4.2]. It also has the smallest Lipschitz constant in value magnitude [Remark 4.7].

### Goal: How is it estimated cheaply, and does one sampled subset inform many points?
MSR: sample m subsets i.i.d.; for each i, estimate phi(i) as the mean utility of sampled subsets containing i minus the mean of those not containing i (Eq 5). Each utility evaluation is reused for every point, giving O(n/ε² log(n/δ)) calls for ℓ2 and O(1/ε² log(n/δ)) for ℓ∞, a factor n better than simple Monte Carlo [Section 4.3, Theorems 4.8-4.9]. The MSR trick does not extend to Shapley or other known semivalues [Appendix C.2].
