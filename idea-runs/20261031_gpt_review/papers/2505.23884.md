# Test-Time Training Done Right

## What this paper does
LaCT (Large-Chunk TTT) updates a nonlinear SwiGLU fast-weight MLP once per very large chunk (2K-1M tokens). Each update takes the gradient of a summed per-token loss over the chunk and applies an optimizer step followed by L2 weight normalization. Large chunks give high GPU utilization and let the fast-weight state grow to 40% of model parameters. A sliding-window attention branch handles local context. The method is demonstrated on novel-view synthesis, language modeling (760M) and autoregressive video diffusion (up to 14B).

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What inner loss and update rule does LaCT use, and does it ever solve any layer in closed form?
Fast weights W = {W1, W2, W3}, f_W(x) = W2[SiLU(W1 x) o (W3 x)] (Eq 6). The loss is a dot product, L = -f_W(k)^T v (Eq 7), not squared error. Update: g = grad_W sum_i eta_i L(f_W(k_i), v_i) over the chunk (Eq 4), then W <- L2-Normalize(W - g) or L2-Normalize(W - Muon(g)), with Muon(g) ≈ U V^T (Eq 8-10) [full text, Section 3.1-3.2]. There is no explicit weight decay and no closed-form solve of any layer. All three matrices take one first-order (Muon) step per chunk. Muon "consistently" outperforms GD and GD with momentum as the test-time optimizer [Section 5.4, Fig 7b]. A nonlinear fast weight beats a linear one even when the linear one has a larger state [Section 5.4, Fig 8a]. In LM, a linear large-chunk variant at equal state size underperforms GLA-SWA, attributed to language lacking chunk structure [Section 5.4, Fig 8b].
