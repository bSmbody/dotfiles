# Human-inspired Episodic Memory for Infinite Context LLMs (EM-LLM)

## What this paper does
EM-LLM segments the token stream into episodic events at points of high surprise (token negative log-likelihood above a moving threshold) and refines the boundaries with graph modularity or conductance on key similarity. At inference it retrieves events by k-NN similarity plus a temporal contiguity buffer, keeping initial tokens and a local window. It reports gains over InfLLM and RAG on long-context benchmarks. Surprise-based segmentation aligns with human event boundaries better than fixed-size chunks.

## Goal-by-goal answers

### Goal: How does it use surprise to define content units, and why?
A token x_t is a candidate event boundary if −log P(x_t | x_<t) > T, with T = mu + gamma × sigma over a moving window [full text, Section 3.2, Eq 1]. The rationale is that "unexpected or novel information plays a crucial role in human memory formation", so surprise is a proxy for significant events. Boundaries are then refined to maximize intra-event key similarity [Section 3.3].

### Goal: What evidence shows surprise-based units are better than fixed ones?
On human-annotated podcast transcripts, surprise-only segmentation scores close to human event boundaries on cohesion and separation metrics, and fixed segmentation does worse (InfLLM's fixed method is worse than random) [Section 4.2, Fig 4]. On PG-19, surprise plus refinement gives the best event-similarity metrics, and fixed plus refinement does not catch up [Section 4.3]. LongBench ablations over gamma are in Appendix D.2.

### Goal: Does it evict KV, or keep everything and retrieve?
It keeps all evicted-from-window tokens as episodic events (offloaded to CPU memory when large) and retrieves some of them into the context at each step [Section 3.1, 3.4; Appendix C.3]. It never decides what to delete under a fixed budget, so surprise is used to segment, not to value or retain.
