# Estimation of relative efficiency of adaptive cluster vs traditional sampling designs applied to arrivals of sharks

## What this paper does
Compares Adaptive Cluster Sampling (ACS) with simple random sampling (SRS) for estimating a sparse, spatially clustered population (thresher-shark landings at California marinas). ACS draws an initial random sample and, whenever a sampled unit satisfies a condition (e.g. y > C), adds its whole neighborhood, iterating until no new unit qualifies. The paper restates Thompson's variance ratio, derives an inequality for when ACS beats SRS, and simulates populations with varying cluster tightness to show how "rare and clustered" the population must be.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is the ACS mechanism and why does it beat non-adaptive sampling on rare, clustered populations?
An initial sample of n1 units is drawn; if unit i meets the condition, all adjacent units are added, and this repeats for newly added units, forming networks [full text, Section 2]. The variance ratio var(ACS)/var(SRS) = (m/n1)((N-n1)/(N-m))[1 - within-network SS / total SS], so ACS wins when most of the population's variation sits inside networks, i.e. when positives occur in patches [Section 4, Eq 1-3]. If n1 = m, ACS always has lower variance than SRS [Section 4, observation 1]. ACS also adapts to clusters without knowing in advance where they are, which a traditional cluster design cannot [Section 1].

### Goal: Under what conditions does ACS lose its advantage?
Relative precision rises as data become more clustered, falls as the condition-to-adapt rises, and falls as the hit level rises (more positives means less gain) [Section 5, items 1-3]. In simulation, tight, widely spaced clusters give about 2x efficiency; for diffuse clusters (SD 2-3 units) the gain is only 40-50%, and some replications gave only 20%, possibly not worth ACS's extra cost and complexity [Section 6, Table 2]. ACS's final sample size is random and cannot be fixed in advance [Section 7]. So ACS pays off only when positives are rare and patchy; for randomly or uniformly spread units it "would perform less well" [Section 7].
