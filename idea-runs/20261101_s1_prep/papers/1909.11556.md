# Reducing Transformer Depth on Demand with Structured Dropout (LayerDrop)

## What this paper does
Introduces LayerDrop: during training, whole Transformer layers (a structured group of weights) are dropped at random. This regularizes training and makes the network robust to removing those structures at inference, so sub-networks of any depth can be extracted from one trained model without fine-tuning. Evaluated on MT, language modeling, summarization, long-form QA and BERT-style pretraining.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is trained, and why does training with random structured drops make inference-time pruning safe?
Random structured dropout samples a {0,1} mask constant over predefined groups (heads, FFN, layers) and applies it during training; layers are the main target [full text, Section 3.2.1]. Training with missing layers makes the network "more robust to predicting with missing layers"; a standard Transformer pruned without LayerDrop "performs poorly as it was not trained to be robust to missing layers", whereas the LayerDrop model pruned to any depth beats models trained from scratch at that depth, with no fine-tuning [Sections 3.2.2, 5.2, Figure 2]. The drop rate should match the target pruning level, p* = 1 − r/N; larger training drop rates help when heavy pruning is planned, which "equalizes the train and test time settings" [Section 3.2.2, Section 6, Figure 6].

### Goal: Does it matter which structures are dropped and how the ones to remove are chosen at inference?
Dropping heads alone is worse than dropping sub-layers or layers [Section 6, Figure 4 left]. At inference, a fixed "every other" rule is hard to beat; validation search and learned per-layer drop rates give only marginal gains, while dropping consecutive blocks (first or last half) is harmful [Sections 3.2.2, 6, Figure 4 right]. So the training-time drop distribution, not the inference-time selection rule, carries most of the effect.
