### C1 — Uplift triggers: learn the frontier call's treatment effect with an X-learner over free cheap rollouts and a few randomized calls

- **Intuition**: Treat a frontier call as a scarce medical-style "treatment" and train the escalation trigger as an uplift model in the style of an X-learner. Abundant, free cheap-VLA rollouts give the "no-call" outcome at every state. A few hundred randomized frontier calls give "called" outcomes, and the difference between each one and the no-call value at the same state is that call's imputed effect. The trigger regresses that imputed effect. It then stays silent on states the cheap policy will finish anyway ("sure things") and on states already past the point of no return ("lost causes"), and fires only on states the helper can still save ("persuadables").

- **Gap it attacks**: Axis "failure prediction signals for cheap VLA policies", Gap 1, with Gap 2 of the same axis as the binding constraint. AEGIS, SAFE, TDQC, Foresight and ReAP regress the cheap policy's own failure probability [2606.06660, 2506.09937, 2604.20472, 2606.23085, 2610.16309]. That score stays high after the recoverability horizon, where the helper's advantage has already collapsed. RESCUE puts that horizon a median 34 steps before the visible drop and finds that real monitors alarm after it in most episodes [2610.13516]. So a risk threshold wastes calls on lost states and fires late on savable ones. AEGIS recovers only about a third of the always-strong headroom, and its edge vanishes in the hard tercile [2606.06660]. The only helper-conditioned estimators today use dense Monte-Carlo branching of the helper (RESCUE, 128K branches [2610.13516]) or abundant backup rollouts (VAPS [2610.01397]), which is unaffordable when each helper rollout is a billed frontier call. Closing the gap would make the problem's "confidently grasps the wrong object" state the place the trigger fires, before the grasp, and spend a small call budget only where it changes the outcome.

- **Source inspiration**: Heterogeneous-treatment-effect estimation and uplift modeling from causal ML and marketing. The X-learner [1706.03461] estimates the conditional treatment effect when one arm is huge and the other tiny. It learns the complex control response from the large control arm. It then subtracts that fitted response from each scarce treated outcome to impute a per-unit effect, and regresses only that residual effect. So the few treated units are spent on the effect, which is often zero or simple, rather than on the full outcome surface. The authors prove that this reaches the effect's own (faster) learning rate when control data are abundant, and show large gains in a 1%-treated simulation where the effect is a simple indicator inside a complex 20-dimensional response [1706.03461, Sections "X-learner", "Smoothness conditions", Appendix A.1]. Fernández-Loría and Provost [2104.04103] say exactly when targeting by outcome risk, the non-causal proxy every VLA trigger uses, is good enough: when risk and effect are correlated. They also say when it breaks: when the treatment can harm some units, or when effect and outcome decouple. They argue that the targeting rule should be judged by decision regret, which randomized data let one estimate directly [2104.04103, Sections 3.1, 3.3]. Novelty type: recombination-of-distant-concepts. A causal-inference estimator built for cheap administrative control data and expensive field treatments is carried over to cheap-VLA rollouts and expensive frontier calls.

- **How it maps to this problem**: A "unit" is a state at a chunk boundary of a cheap-VLA episode. "Control" means letting the cheap VLA continue, and "treatment" means one frontier call at that state (the helper plans or takes over for a fixed span, then hands back), with eventual task success as the outcome. The control response function is the cheap policy's own success value. It is learned from free simulated rollouts with the TD calibration of TDQC [2604.20472] on frozen VLA features, the same features AEGIS probes [2606.06660], and it can be sharpened at any specific state by restoring the simulator checkpoint and rolling the cheap policy out again for free, as RESCUE does [2610.13516]. The treated arm comes from a budgeted data-collection phase. In each training episode the frontier model is called once, at a chunk boundary drawn at random with a known probability that may be skewed toward moderately risky states. Because the call times are randomized by design, the propensity is known and there is no confounding. Each called episode's outcome minus the control value at the call state is an imputed helper advantage. A small head on the same VLA features regresses it, and it is trained to rank states for the call/no-call decision (the regret view of [2104.04103]) rather than to estimate the effect exactly. At deployment the cheap VLA runs, and the trigger calls the frontier model when the predicted advantage, not the risk, clears the price of a call or the remaining per-episode cap. A lost state has a high risk and a low control value, but it gets a near-zero imputed advantage, because the called outcomes there also fail. So it never pays for a call.

- **Why it could work / feasibility**: The X-learner's two preconditions plausibly hold here. (i) The design is extremely unbalanced. Cheap-VLA rollouts in LIBERO-style simulation cost only GPU time, and RESCUE shows checkpoint-restore branching of the cheap policy at scale on LIBERO [2610.13516]. Frontier calls are the scarce arm, so the first-stage error is nearly zero, which is the regime where [1706.03461] proves the fast rate. (ii) The effect is structurally simpler than the outcome surface. RESCUE finds most failures are unrecoverable under any intervention, and recoverability is confined to a window before a horizon [2610.13516], so the helper's advantage is zero on most states and nonzero in a narrow band. That is the "CATE is often zero" structure the X-learner exploits. [2104.04103] predicts that risk targeting breaks exactly when effects decouple from outcomes or can be negative. Both are documented in-domain: risk stays high past the horizon [2610.13516], and well-timed subgoal-rewrite calls lowered LIBERO success from 8/10 to 4/10 on one task [2610.21160]. VAPS independently confirms that conditioning on the backup's own outcome, not the nominal policy's, is what makes switching work [2610.01397]. Labels for the scarce arm fit the budget. RouteLLM bought ~120K judged pairwise labels for ~US$700 [2406.18665], and here a few hundred to ~2,000 single frontier calls, each paired with free cheap continuations, are within the ~US$1,000 cap. A local stronger VLA (π0.5, as in AEGIS [2606.06660]) can stand in as the helper while the pipeline is debugged. Key assumption: the frozen-VLA features that predict cheap-policy risk also carry the information that separates "still savable" from "already lost" (e.g. a wrong-object grasp in progress vs. an object already in the drawer), so a small effect head can learn the band from hundreds of noisy binary outcomes.

- **Novelty vs. closest in-domain work**: AEGIS [2606.06660] is the closest. It is a weak-to-strong VLA cascade gated by a probe trained on the weak policy's eventual failure, with no helper outcomes in its target. C1 trains the gate on the imputed helper effect, using randomized helper calls and the cheap-policy value as the control arm. RouteLLM-style routers [2406.18665] do learn a helper-conditioned win probability, but for single-turn queries with both models labelled on every query, and with no sequential timing or point of no return. RESCUE [2610.13516] and VAPS [2610.01397] estimate helper-conditioned value by dense helper rollouts and train no sparse-label trigger.

- **Main risk**: With only hundreds of binary called outcomes, the imputed effects may be too noisy for the effect head to out-rank the high-signal risk score. This is the failure mode [2104.04103] reports, where an outcome model targets as well as an uplift model. In that case the trigger would collapse back to risk thresholding, gaining nothing at small call budgets.
