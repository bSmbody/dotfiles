# Removing Hidden Confounding by Experimental Grounding

## What this paper does
Kallus, Puli and Shalit combine a large confounded observational sample with a small randomized experiment to estimate the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). They first fit an effect function ω̂ on the large sample with any CATE learner (biased by hidden confounding), then fit only a low-capacity correction η(x) = τ(x) − ω(x) on the small randomized sample, using the known-propensity signed reweighting q(X)Y as an unbiased per-unit effect target. The final estimate is τ̂ = ω̂ + η̂. They prove consistency when η is parametric (linear) and identifiable on the experiment, and show gains on a simulation and on the Tennessee STAR class-size RCT with artificially introduced confounding.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What is the core mechanism and why does it work when the experimental sample is small?
Two steps [full text, Section 3, Eq 1-2, Algorithm 1]. (1) Learn ω̂ on the large sample; it carries the complex shape of the effect but is biased. (2) On the randomized sample, regress q(X_i)Y_i − ω̂(X_i) on θᵀX_i, where q = T/e − (1−T)/(1−e) uses the known propensity; Lemma 1 shows E[q(X)Y | X] = τ(X), so the residual target is unbiased for η. Why it works: "rather than assuming that effects themselves have a parametric structure … we only assume that this hidden confounding function has a parametric structure that we can extrapolate" [Section 1]; the scarce unbiased data are spent only on a simple correction, and the large biased sample supplies the effect's shape, including in regions the experiment does not cover. If the large sample is in fact unbiased, the method learns η ≈ 0 and does no harm [Section 3].

### Goal: What assumptions must hold, and what happens when they fail?
Theorem 1 [Section 4]: (1) ω̂ consistent for ω on the large sample; (2) the large sample's covariates cover the experiment's (one-way overlap; can be relaxed to a subset where η is identifiable); (3) η parametric (linear in the theorem; any finite-parameter family identifiable on the experiment); (4) non-singular design on the experiment; (5) finite fourth moments; (6) strong treatment overlap only in the experiment (trivial for an RCT). Rate: O_p(r(n) + 1/n), i.e. governed by how well ω is learned from the big sample plus a parametric 1/n term for η. If η is not in the parametric family, the extrapolated correction is misspecified; the paper does not test that failure.

### Goal: Does the paper consider the case where the large sample's intervention differs from the experiment's?
Only as future work: "Another direction includes cases where the outcomes or interventions are not directly comparable, but where the difference can be modeled", with surrogate short-term vs. long-term outcomes as the example [Section 6]. The experiments use the same treatment in both samples; the bias is from confounding and population shift [Sections 5.1, 5.2]. In the STAR experiment the 2-step method (RF or ridge base) beats regressions fit on the experiment alone or the confounded sample alone, with the gap growing as the experimental fraction grows from 0.1 to 0.5 [Section 5.2, Fig 2].
