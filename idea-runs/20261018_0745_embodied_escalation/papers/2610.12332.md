# Prediction-Powered Data Fusion for Treatment Effect Estimation

## What this paper does
Jung and Yang fuse a small RCT with a large, possibly confounded observational sample (OBS) for the ATE and CATE. Their principle is to keep the RCT's unbiased target and borrow power from the OBS through two channels: OBS outcome regressions blended into the trial's AIPW score (weight λ), and OBS covariates used as a prediction-powered control term (weight ω), with a calibrated density ratio for covariate shift. For the CATE they build DR-Fusion (DR-learner pseudo-outcome) and R-Fusion (R-learner loss), selecting (λ, ω) by held-out validation, with guarantees relative to the trial-only learner.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: Does it need a parametric model of the gap between the large-sample effect and the true effect, as Kallus et al. (2018) does?
No. The related-work table classes Kallus et al. [K18] among methods that "keep the trial's target only if the OBS bias follows their model", whereas DRF and RF "keep the trial's target under any OBS confounding" and "need no model of the discrepancy between the trial and OBS-based CATEs" [full text, Section 1.1, Table 2]. The OBS enters only through a blended regressor μ̂_λ inside the trial's pseudo-outcome, and E_R[Z^λ|X] = τ_0(X) for every λ ∈ [0,1] [Section 4, Lemma 1].

### Goal: How large is the gain over trial-only CATE learners, and when does it shrink?
DRF and RF reach 0.54–0.71 of the risk of cross-fitted trial-only DR/R-learners in all 45 cells; under a misspecified shift 0.52–0.62 [Section 5.5]. At large trial n the gain comes mostly from the OBS regression used as a basis function rather than from the fusion loss (0.98–1.03 of a same-sieve trial fit at n=3,000) [Section 5.5, "Same sieve"]. Under a strong shift with large heterogeneity, fusion can be slightly worse than its λ-only variant [Section 5.5]. The two samples always share the same treatment; differing treatments are not studied.
