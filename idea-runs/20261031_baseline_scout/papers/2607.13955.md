# Pack, Remove, Reserve -- Online Knapsack with Second Thoughts

## What this paper does
Online proportional knapsack (capacity 1, items arrive one by one, no knowledge of the future) with two paid recourses: reserving an item to decide later at cost alpha*x, and removing an already packed item at cost beta*y. Gain = final packed size minus all recourse costs. The paper gives matching upper and lower competitive-ratio bounds over the whole (alpha, beta) plane and finds three regimes: reservation alone optimal, removal alone optimal, and a central "symbiosis" region where an algorithm that first postpones commitment and then packs and revises beats both.

## Goal-by-goal answers
### Goal: What do reservation and removal mean, what do they cost, and why are they needed at all?
Without recourse the problem is hopeless: "a single tiny item forces a dilemma—pack it and a later item of size 1 no longer fits, or reject it and the sequence ends", so no deterministic online algorithm has a bounded ratio [full text, Section 1]. Reserving lets the algorithm "postpone a decision" at proportional cost alpha*x; reserved items "may be packed at any later time, including after the last arrival". Removing lets it "revise a decision" at cost beta*y, but "rejected and removed items cannot be used later" [Section 1.1]. The obstacle the recourses relax is "the irrevocability of decisions" [Section 1.2].
### Goal: What is the optimal algorithm in the symbiosis region and what is its structure?
Algorithm Symb [Section 2, Algorithm 1] has two phases. Phase 1 reserves every arriving item, "deliberately postponing commitment until a sufficiently large item of size at least p_safe(r) arrives"; the threshold p_safe(r) (Eq 11) falls as the reserve r grows, so the more has already been paid for postponement the sooner commitment starts. Phase 2 stops reserving; for each item it checks whether packing it with a subset of the reserve, removing packed items if needed, already secures the target gain net of costs alpha*r + beta*(p - p'); otherwise it packs greedily or rejects. An invariant keeps the whole reserve packable (p + r <= 1). The paper calls it "a natural blend" of the optimal reservation-only and removal-only algorithms: "postponing commitment through reservation and revising decisions through removal" [Section 2].
### Goal: What decides which regime applies?
Only the two prices. At alpha = 0 reserve everything and decide at the end (ratio 1); at alpha >= 1 reservation never pays and the problem reduces to removal only [Theorem 1 proof sketch]. Near the equilibrium border between the single-mechanism ratios (Eq 4-5), and for 1/4 < alpha < phi - 1 with beta in a band (Eq 6), both must be combined and the optimal ratio 1/f(alpha, beta) (Eqs 7-9) is strictly better than either alone [Section 1.3, Lemma 7]. Where one mechanism is cheap or one is expensive, the border is sharp and one mechanism dominates [Section 3].
### Goal: What assumptions does the result rely on?
Worst-case (adversarial) inputs, proportional values (value = size), costs linear in item size and constant over time, a single knapsack, and an algorithm that is told when the input ends [Section 1.1]. The numbers do not transfer to a stochastic, structured stream; the qualitative structure (price-dependent choice between postponing and revising, with a commitment threshold that depends on how much reserve has been paid for) is what can transfer.
